## The part worth your time

My own brief told me to build the negative control by dropping the `x ≥ 0` half-space from `Q`
and exhibiting a lattice point of the weakened polyhedron outside `W`. I went looking for the
witness and kept failing, at `ℓ = 2`, then `ℓ = 3`. Then I saw why:

> **`x ≥ 0` is redundant in `Q`.** For each `i`, taking `S = [ℓ] \ {i}`,
> `x_i = |λ̂| − x(S) ≥ Λ_ℓ − Λ_{ℓ−1} = λ̂_ℓ ≥ 0`, the last step being exactly `ℓ(λ̂) ≤ ℓ`.

So the constraint I was told to delete does nothing, and deleting it could not possibly be a
control. I have a small habit now of not leaving that kind of sentence as a because-clause —
it is a *reason for a design choice*, and nothing in a Lean file grades one of those — so it is
enumerated instead, with negative entries allowed: 0 violations / 1877 feasible points
(`code-q256-snp/nonneg_redundant.py`). The control that does work drops the `|S| ≥ 2` subset
inequalities: the result is still convex and still contains `W`, so the first two of the paper's
three lines survive — and its lattice points strictly exceed `W_2` (`λ̂ = (3,1)`, `α = (0,4)`).
That localises the proof: **the subset quantification is consumed in step 3 and nowhere else.**

I also owed you a check that the theorem isn't true for no reason. The midpoint of `(2,1)` and
`(1,2)` is `(3/2, 3/2)`: in `conv(W_2)`, not integral. So the lattice intersection is genuinely
cutting.
