## 1. My object has a standard-language name, and it bridges three of the five seed paths

$$g_e(t) = M\bigl[P_{(e)}(X;-t)\bigr] \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
R_e(t) \text{ is the \textbf{connected truncation} of multiplication by } P_{(e)}(X;-t).$$

Multiplication by the one-row **Hall–Littlewood** polynomial. Verified at primary-source level:
Konvalinka `1101.5250` matches **exactly** — no scalar, no conjugation — his §7 stating verbatim
that the quantum power sums *are* Hall–Littlewood $P_r$; the height grading on broken strips is

cross-check is $q_K=q_{HR}^{-2}$, the standard Hecke-vs-HL relation, and the anchor $t=-1$
recovers my already-proved $R_e(-1)=M_{p_e}$.

SEED.md's object in the **Integrable Lattice path** (Wheeler–Zinn-Justin) *and* in the
**Cylindric path** — your masters thesis. So this is the first time the ribbon work has had a
route back into the lattice models. The concrete question (my Q86): $g_e$ has a vertex model
(spin Hall–Littlewood), so **is the component count $m$ a path statistic in it, and is
$(1+t)^{m-1}$ a Boltzmann weight?** If yes, the entire $(1+t)$-divisibility programme —

## 2. A structural gap in the loop, not a lapse

Three of today's five sessions — **BROWSE, LEAN and REVIEW** — shipped real results and left
**no entry in `SUMMARY.md`**. That includes the Hall–Littlewood crown above. DREAM 09-04 found
the identical gap for REVIEW 09-04.

It isn't forgetfulness. `clio.log` captures every session's closing summary automatically;
`SUMMARY.md` is written by hand and only WAKE, PROVE and DREAM do it. So **`clio.log` is the
complete record and `SUMMARY.md` is a lossy digest** — and since `SUMMARY.md` is what the next
WAKE reads, a silent session's findings are invisible to the entire following cycle. I've
reconstructed all three tonight and made "diff SUMMARY headers against clio.log" the first step
of every DREAM.

If you ever want to know whether a session found something, `state/clio.log` is the source of
truth, not `SUMMARY.md`. Worth knowing before you read either.

## 3. A methodological result I'm pleased with

Six times today, in four sessions sharing no mathematics, a **pinned coordinate manufactured the
phenomenon**. The nice part is that the two symptoms turn out to be one defect: pin a coordinate
and then *test*, you get a check that can't fail; pin it and then *state*, you get a hypothesis
describing the pin. Rick's $E_3=0$ slice exhibited both at once — a verification structurally
incapable of failing, and a true identity that appeared to fail.

And it has a control, which is what makes it usable rather than just cautionary. The Lean work
showed a paper's hypothesis $\rho(1)=m<k$ is genuinely **load-bearing** — dropping it would give
a clean theorem wrong at its own boundary. So the test isn't "is there a fixed parameter?" but
**"do the proofs of my own lemmas actually use it?"**
